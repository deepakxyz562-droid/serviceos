If you're shopping for software to run your service business, you'll encounter two categories that sound similar: CRM (Customer Relationship Management) and FSM (Field Service Management). Vendors use the terms loosely, sometimes interchangeably, which makes it hard to figure out what you actually need.

This guide explains the difference, what each is built for, and how to choose — or, more commonly, how to get both in one platform.

## What is CRM software?

CRM software manages your relationship with customers and prospects. It's the system of record for:

- **Contact information** — names, phone numbers, email addresses, job addresses
- **Communication history** — every call, email, text, and meeting
- **Sales pipeline** — leads, opportunities, quotes, win/loss tracking
- **Customer lifecycle** — when they became a customer, what they've bought, renewal dates

CRM originated in sales-driven industries — B2B software, real estate, financial services — where the core workflow is: find a lead, nurture the relationship, close a deal, manage the account. Salesforce, HubSpot, and Zoho CRM are the best-known examples.

A pure CRM answers questions like:
- Who are my customers and how do I reach them?
- What's in my sales pipeline?
- When did we last talk to this customer?
- What's the lifetime value of this account?

## What is field service management software?

FSM software manages the operational side of a service business — the work performed in the field. It's the system of record for:

- **Scheduling and dispatch** — who's doing what job, when, and where
- **Technician management** — routes, skills, availability, job assignments
- **Job tracking** — job status, work orders, photos, signatures, parts used
- **Invoicing and payments** — job-based billing, on-site payment collection
- **Asset and service history** — which equipment is at which customer, what's been done to it

FSM originated in industries where work happens at customer locations — plumbing, HVAC, electrical, cleaning, landscaping. ServiceTitan, Jobber, Housecall Pro, and ServiceOS are examples.

A pure FSM answers questions like:
- Which technician is closest to this emergency call?
- What's on today's schedule for technician #3?
- What parts were used on the Johnson job last month?
- Has the customer paid for the water heater repair?

## Where they overlap (and confuse people)

The confusion arises because every FSM system includes some CRM functionality, and many CRM systems have added light FSM features. Here's the overlap:

| Feature | Pure CRM | Pure FSM | Both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contact database | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Communication history | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Lead/opportunity tracking | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ (basic) |
| Quote generation | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Scheduling/dispatch | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Technician mobile app | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Work orders | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Parts/inventory | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Job-based invoicing | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Asset/service history | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Sales pipeline forecasting | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| Marketing automation | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |

The features in the "Both" column — contacts, communication history, quotes — are why vendors can claim to be "a CRM" even if they're really an FSM platform. And the features unique to each side are what actually matter when choosing.

## Which do you need?

The honest answer for most service businesses: **both, ideally in one platform.**

Here's how to think about it based on your business type.

### If you're a trades contractor (plumber, HVAC, electrician, etc.)

You need FSM. Your core workflow is: get a call, schedule a technician, dispatch them with the right parts, complete the job, invoice. That's field service management.

You also need CRM functionality — customer history, communication logs, quotes — but it's secondary to the operational workflow. Choose an FSM platform that has solid built-in CRM features (contact management, job history per customer, quote tracking) rather than buying separate FSM and CRM tools that don't talk to each other.

A plumber running ServiceTitan for dispatch and Salesforce for CRM would have customer data split across two systems, with no link between a customer's service history (in ServiceTitan) and their sales pipeline (in Salesforce). That's a mess.

### If you're a B2B service provider (IT services, consulting, commercial maintenance)

You may lean more toward CRM. Your workflow is: find prospects, nurture relationships, close contracts, manage accounts. Field service — if any — is a smaller component (maybe you send a technician for installations, but the core business is the account relationship).

In this case, a CRM like HubSpot or Salesforce is the right primary system, with FSM features added only if field work is a meaningful part of your business.

### If you're a home services business (cleaning, landscaping, pest control)

You need FSM with strong recurring-schedule capabilities. Your workflow revolves around recurring jobs — weekly mowing, monthly pest treatments, quarterly cleanings — and the CRM functionality you need is basic: who's the customer, what's their schedule, what's their payment status.

An FSM platform built for recurring service businesses is the right choice. Pure CRM would be overkill and wouldn't handle scheduling.

### If you're a construction or project-based contractor

You need a hybrid. Large projects (a $50,000 kitchen remodel, a $200,000 commercial buildout) involve both account management (CRM) and field execution (FSM). You'll likely need:

- Project management for multi-phase jobs
- CRM for client relationship management
- FSM for subcontractor scheduling and field work
- Estimating and progress billing

Many construction businesses end up with specialized construction management software (BuilderTrend, CoConstruct) rather than either pure CRM or pure FSM.

## Why most service businesses should avoid running separate CRM and FSM

The temptation is to buy "best of breed" — a top CRM for sales and a top FSM for operations. This rarely works well for service businesses, for three reasons:

**Data duplication.** The customer record lives in both systems, and they drift. The CRM says the customer's address is 123 Oak St; the FSM says 125 Oak St. Which is right? Nobody knows.

**Workflow gaps.** A lead in the CRM becomes a customer who needs a job scheduled in the FSM. If they're separate systems, someone has to manually create the customer in the FSM, then manually link the job back to the CRM opportunity. This handoff breaks constantly.

**Reporting blindness.** You can't answer basic business questions: "Which marketing source produces the highest-lifetime-value customers?" requires data from both the CRM (lead source) and the FSM (job revenue). If they're separate, the answer requires manual data merging in a spreadsheet.

For most service businesses, a unified platform that does both — FSM with built-in CRM — is simpler, cheaper, and produces better data.

## What to look for in a unified platform

If you're shopping for a platform that handles both CRM and FSM for a service business, evaluate these capabilities:

**CRM side:**
- Unified customer database with full service history
- Communication logging (calls, emails, SMS all attached to the customer)
- Quote generation and tracking
- Lead source tracking (so you know which marketing produces customers)

**FSM side:**
- Drag-and-drop dispatch calendar with technician views
- Mobile technician app that works offline
- Work orders with photos, signatures, and parts tracking
- Recurring schedule support (for maintenance contracts)
- Job-based invoicing with on-site payment collection
- Asset/equipment history per customer

**Integration side:**
- The CRM and FSM share ONE customer record — no sync, no duplication
- Every job is linked to a customer, and every customer shows their job history
- Communication about a job (SMS reminders, follow-ups) is logged on both the customer and the job

## The bottom line

CRM and FSM solve different problems, but for service businesses they're two halves of the same workflow: find customers (CRM), serve them in the field (FSM), get paid, and repeat. The question isn't which one you need — it's whether to run them as separate systems or as one unified platform.

For most service businesses, especially small to mid-sized ones, a unified platform is the right answer. It's cheaper, simpler, and produces cleaner data — and clean data is what lets you make good business decisions.
